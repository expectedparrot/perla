January creates three different operating commitments. Starbucks launches the 20% café-capacity reservation and instruments the 50 pilot and 50 comparison cafés. Dutch Bros splits its assumed $30,000 monthly envelope evenly between peak staffing and a geographically targeted $1 Order Ahead offer. Dunkin launches a $6 coffee-and-food bundle in its 15 consenting stores, with a $20,000 cap and a rule to withdraw where economics worsen.

The customer model preserves the distinction between occasions. The habitual app user abandons a slot more than eight minutes later than desired and changes its routine after two failures in a week. The commuter checks availability before choosing a route. The café visitor keeps coming and may visit more often if the service actually improves. These are the model’s contingent choices, not measured segment shares.

Control integrates the eleven actions in one ruling. It anticipates diversion to competitors or home preparation, while explicitly leaving rival conversion rates and capacity unknown. All four players accept when the ruling is returned to them, and control affirms it. Acceptance is a legitimate response: the elicitation does not require a player to invent a dispute. The final ruling remains provisional because its causal effects rely on judgment and there is no human assessment.

The mechanism is more useful than a winner announcement, but it still contains a weak inference. The outcome says café visitors experience improved waits, even though a preference for better service and a capacity reservation do not establish the size—or existence—of that improvement under an actual local queue. All observed metrics remain empty. That claim needs operating evidence before it can support the February service gate.
