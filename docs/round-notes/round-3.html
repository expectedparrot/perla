March exposes the tension between improving an offer and repairing a changed habit. Starbucks expands its dynamic-release rule from 10 to all 50 pilot cafés, retaining the original 20% reservation and staffing cap. Dutch Bros keeps the $1 acquisition offer and adds a local review of lane times, order volume and repeat visits. Dunkin increases its proposed bundle discount to $1.50, splitting its $20,000 envelope equally between labor and marketing/promotional activity.

The customer model now says some formerly habitual Starbucks app users open a rival app by default; café visitors remain attached to the pilot stores, while commuters keep checking the daily pickup promise. This is a concrete strategic risk hypothesis: restoring availability may not immediately restore a routine that a customer has already changed. It is not an estimate of how many real customers would switch, and the model’s confidence about established habits exceeds the operating evidence available here.

Starbucks also proposes a rollback rule if mobile abandonment or café waits worsen by “more than 5 percentage points” against paired controls. That phrasing is usable for a rate such as abandonment, but a wait measured in minutes needs a time-unit or relative-percent threshold. The returned move is preserved exactly. Control’s structural acceptance does not fix the unit ambiguity, and expanding the variation across the pilot still lacks measured support from its ten-store trial.

All four actors accept the integrated March ruling, and control affirms it. The board reaches March and records the expanded pilot variation, rival offers and assumed customer routines. The repeated agreement is a finding about these model responses; it supplies no additional operating evidence. Six judgment rulings across the three rounds remain without human assessments. No final criterion evaluation has been executed, and the required observed waits, contribution and repeat data remain absent.
