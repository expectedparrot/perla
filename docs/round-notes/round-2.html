February gives Starbucks a more specific response than simply continuing the launch. It keeps the 20% reservation at all 50 pilot cafés, then proposes a controlled variation in 10: after 8 a.m., release half of the unused reserved capacity to mobile orders when café demand has used less than 15% of capacity. The intent is to recover mobile availability when the reserved allocation would otherwise sit unused, while preserving the initial staffing and pricing limits.

Dunkin proposes a $1 app discount on a breakfast bundle, allocating $12,000 to morning labor and $8,000 to advertising and promotional incentives. Dutch Bros continues its $15,000 labor and $15,000 acquisition split. The customer model begins February from the prior public outcome: frustrated app users try the rival apps, commuters divert more readily, and café visitors reinforce their in-store routine. The key question becomes whether a more flexible queue rule arrives before those alternative routines become durable.

Control writes four rulings, separating customer behavior and each company’s actions while giving them the same proposed public history. The affected players ultimately accept all four, and control affirms them. Two first attempts at rebuttals are rejected before that point: Dunkin responds to only one of its two assigned rulings, and Starbucks returns an empty list. Fresh calls supply the required explicit responses. Perla does not interpret silence as acceptance.

The service gate is now due, but its evidence is still absent. A plan to instrument stores is not a completed set of wait-time measurements. The new ten-store variation is an operational hypothesis to test, not proof that unused capacity actually exists or that releasing it improves contribution. The final board carries the variation forward while keeping actual wait, contribution and repeat metrics empty.
