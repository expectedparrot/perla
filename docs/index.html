Perla · A worked strategy case

Protect the café queue without giving away the morning customer.

Use a competitive simulation to improve a Starbucks pilot: give rivals a reason to respond, let customers reject the offer, and challenge the explanation of what happens next.

The decision this exercise helps you make

Suppose Starbucks reserves part of its morning production capacity for customers standing in the café. Mobile customers would see later pickup slots when the remaining capacity fills. Faster café service sounds attractive. But a commuter can leave, a rival can subsidize the first visit, and a customer who changes their morning routine may be slow to return.

Our recommendation from this exercise is to test a flexible reservation before expanding a fixed rule. Compare a protected café allocation with one that releases unused capacity to mobile orders. Track the entire purchase journey, including later purchases and no purchase, and measure whether customers return after a rival’s offer ends. A shorter café queue alone is insufficient grounds to expand.

That is an experimental recommendation drawn from the scenario, not a forecast of Starbucks’ results. The three rounds below make the reasoning inspectable: rivals respond to restricted mobile availability, Starbucks adapts its rule, and the customer model raises the possibility that habits persist after service recovers. The useful output is a better pilot design and a sharper question to test.

How a business simulation tests the proposal

Perla organizes a strategy exercise around players who have different interests and limited information. In this case, one player makes decisions for Starbucks, two respond as Dutch Bros and Dunkin, and a fourth chooses what customers would do when faced with those offers. We use language models to play these roles. The customer player is a simulated decision maker working from specified customer situations; its answers are not responses from actual customers.

We give each player a written briefing, called a dossier, describing its background, objectives, resources and limits. The Starbucks player can change the pilot’s staffing and ordering rules. The rival players can choose local offers within their budgets. The customer player can buy coffee elsewhere, make it at home or skip the purchase. Each submits a move: the actions it chooses for that month, without seeing the other players’ current choices.

A separate model then acts as the adjudicator, called control. It reads the combined moves and explains their likely consequences in a ruling. The players get a chance to object before that ruling becomes the starting situation for the next month. This is where red teaming tests the explanation: a rival or customer can point out that an assumed response conflicts with its incentives or available choices. Below, we build those briefings, play a complete month and inspect an actual objection that changed a ruling.

Three kinds of information to keep separate

Reported facts ground the business: company financials, app capabilities and operating models. Scenario assumptions define this hypothetical local game: budgets, store counts, customer time limits. Simulation judgments describe what might follow when the actors interact. Perla checks structure, citations and job lineage; an accepted response can still make a poor business inference.

Perla exports EDSL Jobs and imports EDSL Results. The ep CLI chooses models and runs inference through Expected Parrot. The files preserve what each actor saw and returned. In a facilitated game, people can also supply moves and assess rulings.

Set up the pilot and load its cast

Starbucks’ public strategy combines coffeehouse service, staffing investment and digital order sequencing. Its reported Q3 fiscal 2026 US comparable transactions grew 4.2%; the question here is how to sustain momentum while improving the café experience. The particular reservation policy below is our proposed experiment. Strategy context · Quarterly results.

The case covers January–March 2027: 50 pilot cafés, 50 comparison cafés, a 20% café-capacity reservation on weekdays from 7–10 a.m., unchanged prices and up to two extra staff-hours per pilot café per weekday. It assumes relevant local overlap with Dutch Bros and Dunkin. Confirm that overlap before choosing real stores.

Build the case through commands, or load the supplied files

You can author every part of this case with Perla commands. The complete command walkthrough starts in an empty game directory and builds the decision, three evaluation criteria, shared starting situation and all four sourced dossiers. It finishes with the review policy and rejoins this tutorial before the first model call.

After creating a fresh game directory, the command route begins by turning the pilot proposal into a draft decision:

perla game decision set \
  --statement 'Pilot peak-time mobile-order admission limits in 50 US Starbucks cafés during January–March 2027, reserving 20% of measured production capacity for café orders and offering later pickup slots when the mobile allocation is full.' \
  --offer 'Capacity-aware mobile pickup slots with a protected café-order allocation from 7–10 a.m. on weekdays' \
  --segment '50 US company-operated cafés with nearby Dutch Bros and Dunkin alternatives, paired with 50 comparison cafés' \
  --pricing 'Keep beverage prices unchanged; assumed $3 contribution per incremental or lost transaction before added staffing, not a reported company figure' \
  --launch-period 'January 2027; a proposed experiment, not an announced Starbucks policy' \
  --scope '50 pilot cafés and 50 comparison cafés; reserve 20% of measured 15-minute capacity for café orders at peak; no automatic transfer of the policy to other stores' \
  --moves 3 \
  --months-per-move 1

Follow the remaining construction steps to add the criteria and initial board, inspect the draft with perla game show, and register it with perla init. You then use actor add and the dossier commands to build each player. This route makes the design choices explicit as you go.

The file-loading route below imports the same design in fewer commands. Choose it if you want to inspect the prepared inputs and start playing quickly. Both routes produce the same case; use a fresh game directory for whichever route you choose.

Define the experiment in game.json

Read the supplied game.json. It contains three parts: decision, the proposal being tested; criteria, the conditions for judging it; and board, the starting situation shared with all players. Here is an excerpt showing how the proposal and calendar become inputs:

{
  "decision": {
    "statement": "Pilot peak-time mobile-order admission limits in 50 US Starbucks cafés during January–March 2027, reserving 20% of measured production capacity for café orders and offering later pickup slots when the mobile allocation is full.",
    "moves": 3,
    "months_per_move": 1
  }
}

This excerpt illustrates the fields; the supplied file also specifies the offer, customer segment, pricing and scope. Three moves of one month each give Starbucks two opportunities to adapt after January. The 50-café scope and 20% reservation are choices we made for the experiment. For another strategy question, the case author must make those choices explicitly.

We also wrote three decision criteria before play: seek a 20% reduction in café waiting time by February, nonnegative incremental contribution by March and no more than a three-percentage-point deterioration in repeat purchasing by March. The file specifies comparison groups and minimum sample sizes. These are proposed standards for evaluating a real pilot; a model’s prediction does not satisfy them.

The initial board records January 2027, the participating store counts, the reservation rule and the public market context. Actual wait, contribution and retention measurements start as null, meaning unmeasured. Its measurement_rule tells control to keep hypothetical estimates separate from observations. This is how the case author defines both what everyone knows at the start and what remains unknown.

Initialize Perla from the complete supplied file once you have inspected those choices. This creates the local .perla record and fixes the decision and criteria for this run. The two --delegate options let this model-only walkthrough proceed without a person signing off on dossiers and rulings. For a facilitated exercise, omit them and follow the human-review instructions.

perla init --input case/game.json --delegate dossiers --delegate rulings

Connect the players to their written briefings

players.json is the roster we wrote for this case. Here is the complete file:

{
  "actors": [
    {"id": "home", "name": "Starbucks", "role": "home", "dossier": "home.json"},
    {"id": "dutch", "name": "Dutch Bros", "role": "competitor", "dossier": "dutch.json"},
    {"id": "dunkin", "name": "Dunkin", "role": "competitor", "dossier": "dunkin.json"},
    {"id": "buyer", "name": "Morning customers (composite)", "role": "wildcard", "dossier": "buyer.json"}
  ]
}

The IDs are short names used in later commands. The home role identifies the company whose decision we are testing; competitor identifies its rivals; wildcard lets us include another participant, here customers. Each dossier path points to a briefing beside this roster, so home.json means case/home.json.

Those briefings contain the substance of the players: sourced facts, incentives, constraints, capabilities and limits they must not cross. For example, Starbucks’ briefing cites its quarterly report for segment financials, but labels the $3 transaction contribution and two-hour staffing allowance as author-designed assumptions. The next section explains all four backgrounds and shows how to author dossier entries with Perla commands.

Load the roster and all four complete briefings together:

perla actor load --input case/players.json

This registers the players and records their dossiers in one transaction. It refuses conflicting existing content, so loading the case again cannot silently replace your edits. It has not yet asked a model to choose a move.

Choose how rulings receive review

The final input, live-policy.json, controls review of the adjudicator’s work. We supplied this policy for the automated walkthrough:

{
  "human_budget": 0,
  "auto_confidence": 0.7,
  "judgment_share_threshold": 0.4
}

human_budget allocates zero human reviews for escalated rulings. auto_confidence sets the confidence threshold for automatic eligibility of otherwise unflagged rulings grounded in dossier facts. judgment_share_threshold causes a warning when more than 40% of a closed round’s rulings rest on judgment. These settings govern review; they do not make a confident model correct. Player objections still run, and the record retains unassessed judgments.

perla adjudicate policy set --input case/live-policy.json
perla status

You have now registered the experiment, loaded its players and selected the review policy. The expected data.phase in the status response is move_generation; missing_dossiers is empty. No model has run yet. Editing the copied input files later does not automatically update the registered game; use the dossier commands below to change player briefings, and initialize a fresh game to change the decision or criteria.

Give each player a business—and a limit

The cast file is a short roster linking actor IDs to ordinary dossier files. Bulk loading and direct authoring produce the same dossiers; the complete backgrounds, sources and authoring commands remain available for inspection.

Starbucks: protect service without losing contribution

Starbucks’ coffeehouse history makes the visit part of its proposition. Q3 fiscal 2026 North America revenue was $7,395.1 million and operating margin 13.6%, which establish business scale but tell us little about an individual pilot café. The constructed management tension is between the operations lead’s service and labor goals, the digital lead’s pickup reliability, and finance’s all-channel contribution target.

In the case, Starbucks can adjust pickup windows, staffing and the reservation rule within 50 cafés. It can instrument its own attempts, purchases and repeat visits. It cannot see where every rejected customer ultimately buys. Its assumed $3 transaction contribution and $30 hourly labor cost imply that two extra staff-hours require 20 genuinely incremental transactions per café per day to break even. Sources and complete dossier.

Here is how those economics and operating limits enter the player’s briefing. dossier fact records the claim and its source; dossier constraint makes the staffing and pilot limits explicit. These two entries are already in the loaded case, so repeating them leaves the dossier unchanged.

perla dossier fact home h6 \
  'Assumed economics: $3 contribution per incremental or lost transaction before additional staffing. Assume fully loaded incremental labor costs $30/hour for sensitivity calculations. Two added hours cost $60/store/day and require 20 genuinely incremental transactions to break even before other costs. No actual store margins, baselines or demand elasticities are supplied.' \
  --source 'Author-designed simulation assumption, 2026-09-12; not a company disclosure or measured customer observation.'

perla dossier constraint home \
  'The two-hour staff allowance, unchanged prices and 50-store limit are binding game constraints (h5).'

Inspect the result with perla dossier show home. For the rest of the player’s history, financials, incentives, capabilities and information limits, follow the complete Starbucks construction commands.

Dutch Bros: buy a repeat customer, not another bottleneck

Dutch Bros’ drive-through service and Order Ahead app offer an alternative morning routine. Its Q2 2026 revenue was $550.9 million, with company-operated same-shop transactions up 3.4%. In our constructed backstory, a regional operator is integrating staff while protecting service consistency. The local player controls 12 participating shops and an assumed $30,000 monthly acquisition-and-labor envelope. It can see its own lane times and redemptions, but cannot target a verified list of people rejected inside Starbucks’ app. Sources and complete dossier.

Dunkin: keep the franchisees willing to participate

Dunkin brings a coffee-and-food value proposition and a franchise operating model. Its app and breakfast offers make the whole basket relevant. Our player represents 15 consenting local restaurants with an assumed $20,000 monthly staffing-and-promotion budget. The constructed backstory is disagreement over offers that increased traffic without profitable food attachment. The player can coordinate an opt-in offer and measure its own costs; it cannot impose national pricing or assume corporate reimbursement. Sources and complete dossier.

Customers: the occasion determines the alternative

The customer player represents three assumed occasions: a commuter with ten minutes of slack and an $8 budget; a habitual app user willing to tolerate an eight-minute delay communicated before payment; and a café visitor with a $7 drink budget and 25 minutes to sit. Home preparation, postponement and no purchase are available. These are designed constraints, not measured segment weights or survey responses. The player sees public offers and its own experience, not confidential business plans. Sources and complete dossier.

Adjust assumptions before play

To explore a different budget, make a separate game, revise the relevant fact with --replace, and revise any dependent constraints consistently before exporting moves. Richness should explain authority and incentives; it should not prescribe the response you hope a player will choose. The eight-minute tolerance, for example, is an input to this game. Its reappearance in a model answer is not a discovery.

Define what control can conclude

Control’s default role and response schema are built into Perla. You configure its case evidence through case/game.json, the dossiers and any supplied testimony; you choose its model with ep. There is currently no separate CLI command for replacing control’s role prompt. adjudicate open assembles the frozen evidence after all actors commit.

Set the decision gates before initialization

The supplied game.json defines three gates in criteria. Service requires a 20% adjusted reduction in café p90 wait by February across at least 40 complete store pairs. Economics requires nonnegative adjusted daily contribution by March. Retention requires no worse than a three-percentage-point adjusted repeat decline, with at least 1,000 complete customers per arm. Change these definitions before initializing a different game; the registered criteria are fixed for that run.

The same file’s board.measurement_rule specifies how the public situation is represented: keep absent observations empty, put hypothetical estimates in separate simulated_* fields, and carry observable developments into public_strategy_history. The case notes spell out the measurement definitions. Control sees the full dossiers; private information can leak through its published prose, so inspect the proposed public update before closure.

Inspect the argument, not just the schema

A café-capacity reservation is an action. Lower café waiting time is a consequence requiring assumptions about arrivals, preparation times and use of the reserved capacity. In this case those operating distributions are absent. A good ruling can identify a possible improvement, but its magnitude—and potentially its direction—remains judgment. The prompt requires control to state the basis of each causal link and cite qualified facts such as d.home.h6.

For each important causal link, ask whether the claimed consequence follows from the cited evidence and whether control has excluded a choice available to a player. Structural acceptance ensures the response fits the game; it does not fix a bad causal argument.

What a successful objection looks like

In an earlier, separate run of this Starbucks case, control explained the effects of delayed mobile orders by saying customers would redirect their purchases to Dunkin or Dutch Bros. When that ruling was returned to the customer player, it objected:

The ruling assumes that a delayed mobile user will always switch to a competitor like Dunkin or Dutch Bros. However, some customers may choose to prepare coffee at home, postpone their purchase, or skip it altogether on a given day, especially if a detour to a competitor is inconvenient.

The customer’s briefing explicitly allowed home preparation, postponement and skipping the purchase. Its response cited that entry and identified the disputed part of the ruling: the claim that a delayed mobile order would become a competitor purchase. Control then revised its explanation and the published outcome to include those other choices.

The simulation did not discover those alternatives; we had supplied them. The objection caught control neglecting them. That correction matters for the pilot: a lost Starbucks order cannot simply be credited to a rival, so researchers need to learn where the uncompleted order went. The original ruling, objection and revision preserve the complete exchange from that separate run.

February and March: does adaptation arrive in time?

To execute the remaining rounds, continue with the visible February commands, then the March commands. They repeat the same commitment, ruling, rebuttal and resolution cycle using the preceding round’s closed board. The account below follows the original recorded case.

February: release unused capacity

Starbucks retained the 20% reservation, but tested a variation in ten cafés: after 8 a.m., release half the unused allocation to mobile orders when café demand uses less than 15% of capacity. Dutch Bros kept its $15,000 labor and $15,000 acquisition split. Dunkin proposed a $1 bundle discount with $12,000 for staffing and $8,000 for promotion. The customer model described frustrated app users trying alternatives.

This is the most actionable adaptation in the case. A fixed reservation can leave capacity unused; a conditional release rule gives Starbucks something specific to test. It remains an operational hypothesis because no store measurements arrived between rounds. The service gate was due, but the game could not establish that it passed.

Control produced four rulings, and the actors ultimately accepted them. Two incomplete rebuttals were structurally rejected and replaced by fresh calls. Those are execution failures, not substantive disagreement. The February transcript preserves both attempts.

March: expansion meets an uncertain habit change

Starbucks expanded the conditional release rule from ten to all 50 pilot cafés. Dunkin increased its proposed bundle discount to $1.50, while Dutch Bros continued its offer and reviewed local performance. The customer model said some former Starbucks app users now opened a rival app by default. That persistence is a plausible risk to investigate; the supplied loyalty assumptions and the prior simulated history helped produce it.

Expansion did not follow demonstrated success in the ten-store variation. Starbucks also proposed rollback if mobile abandonment or café waits worsened by “more than 5 percentage points.” That unit can describe a rate, but it does not specify a usable threshold for waiting time measured in minutes. Control accepted the structure without correcting the ambiguity. These are reasons to retain experimental discipline despite a coherent story.

The March transcript ends with agreement and a closed public board. The three real operating gates remain unevaluated. The round progression nevertheless identifies an adaptive rule to test, a rival-response risk and a measurement flaw to correct.

The memo to the pilot team

Recommendation: authorize a bounded comparison of the fixed reservation and conditional release rule, with adequate baseline and comparison data. Do not expand the rule on the strength of a simulated queue improvement. Predefine rollback separately for waiting time, abandonment and contribution, using units that match each metric.

Competitive preparation: assume rivals can deploy local offers quickly through existing channels. Test whether switching persists after a promotion ends. Distinguish an order delayed within Starbucks, an order transferred to another Starbucks channel, a competitor purchase and no purchase. The outside-option challenge shows why these cannot be collapsed into a single lost-sale measure.

First research priority: instrument the rejected-slot journey and follow the baseline mobile cohort across channels. Pair that with café waits, staffing cost and all-channel contribution. Under the case’s assumed unit economics, the full extra labor allowance requires 20 incremental transactions per café per day; a channel shift does not cover that bill.

What would change this recommendation: evidence that reservation capacity is rarely unused would weaken the case for conditional release. Evidence that shorter waits fail to offset mobile losses would favor narrowing or stopping the policy. Persistent rival repeat after offers expire would make recovery of routine a separate intervention. These are testable propositions from the case, not measured conclusions.
